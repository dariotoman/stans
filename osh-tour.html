Before you start walking

Osh claims three thousand years, and celebrated that anniversary in 2000. The claim is generous but the idea behind it is sound: this is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in Central Asia, and the reason is standing in the middle of it. Sulaiman-Too is a limestone ridge rising straight out of the city, a landmark visible from every direction, sacred long before anyone here was Muslim, and a reliable water source at the point where the Ak-Buura river comes down out of the Alay mountains into the Fergana Valley.

That put Osh on a junction. The Silk Road branch coming over the high passes from Kashgar dropped into the valley here. Everything that moved along it — goods, but more importantly Buddhism, then Islam, then Turkic languages — came through this bowl.

The Fergana Valley is a single geographic unit: one flat, fertile, densely populated basin ringed by mountains. In the 1920s and 30s Soviet national delimitation cut it into three republics. The borders were drawn through villages, fields and irrigation systems, and they left large populations on what became, after 1991, the wrong side of an international line. Osh ended up in Kyrgyzstan with a very large Uzbek population and the Uzbekistan border a short drive away. Almost everything distinctive about this city — the food, the courtyard houses, the bazaar, the mosques, and the violence of 1990 and 2010 — runs back to that fact.

One thing to hold in mind

In June 2010, following the overthrow of the government in Bishkek, several days of violence between Kyrgyz and Uzbek communities took place in Osh and Jalal-Abad. Official counts put the dead at roughly 400; other estimates run considerably higher. Hundreds of thousands were displaced and much of the Uzbek mahalla was burned. The 1990 Osh riots, over land allocation, were smaller but not by much.

You will walk past a memorial to this in the first five minutes, and through the neighbourhoods it happened in around Stop 9. Osh today is calm, welcoming and easy to travel in, and people are not walking around thinking about it. The useful posture is simply to know, to read the monuments carefully, and not to ask strangers to narrate it for you. If someone raises it, listen.

1

Osh fell to the Russian Empire in 1876, with the conquest of the Kokand Khanate. What followed was the standard imperial pattern: a garrison, a settler population, a new grid of streets laid out beside the old town rather than through it, and eventually a church. This one went up in 1909, for soldiers and Russian migrants — a small wooden-and-masonry Orthodox building in a city that was already overwhelmingly Muslim and had been for eight centuries.

The three monuments behind it

Take your time here, because the yard behind the church is the densest thing on this walk. Mother's Tears commemorates those killed in the June 2010 interethnic clashes. A second monument is for the Chernobyl liquidators — the Soviet Union conscripted clean-up workers from every republic, and several thousand went from Kyrgyzstan; many came home ill. The Grieving Mother is for the dead of the Second World War.

Three griefs, one small yard, and only one of them is about a war this city fought in. An empire's war in Europe, a reactor failure three thousand kilometres away, and a week of violence in these streets sixteen years ago — all commemorated in the same idiom, all with the mother as the figure who carries it.

Note

The church has been undergoing repair work recently and may be partly scaffolded or the interior closed. The monuments are outside and always accessible.

2

Reportedly the largest Lenin statue in Central Asia, and — the part that matters — it is still standing exactly where it was built, on the central square, at full ceremonial scale. Compare Bishkek, where in 2003 the Lenin was quietly walked round to the back of the museum. In Osh he never moved.

Why that's not just inertia

Southern Kyrgyzstan's relationship with the Soviet period is warmer than the north's, and older residents of both communities will tell you that the USSR was the era when Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in this valley did not kill each other. Whether that memory is accurate in every respect is arguable; the fact that it is widely held is not. A monument left in place is a statement about the present, not the past.

Look for

What surrounds him now. Soviet ceremonial squares were designed for parades toward a single focal point; watch how the space is actually used on a summer evening, and by whom.

3

Soviet monumental mosaic did a specific job: put the message on the largest available wall, in a medium that survives sun, and let the local visual repertoire in through the back door. Get close enough to see the individual tiles — smalti, hand-cut coloured glass, set at slight angles so the whole surface shifts as you walk past it.

The two subjects

The horse is the easy one: the foundation of Kyrgyz nomadic life, transport, wealth, food, sport and metaphor all at once. Even in a mosaic about industrial modernity it stays.

The women are the harder one. Female emancipation was one of the Soviet state's loudest claims in Central Asia, and it was pushed hard from the 1920s — unveiling campaigns, literacy drives, women into paid work and public office. Some of it was genuinely transformative and is remembered that way by the women who benefited. It was also coercive, deeply resented in places, and in the early years got women killed by their own communities. A mosaic like this shows you the triumphant version. It's worth knowing there was another.

Note

4

The second and larger of the two mosaics, wrapping the theatre. This was standard practice for late-Soviet cultural buildings: rather than ornament the facade, replace it — one continuous composition at the scale of the whole wall, legible from across a road.

Theatre mattered enormously in the Soviet cultural programme in Central Asia, for the same reason opera did in Bishkek. Regional drama theatres were built everywhere, staffed by graduates of Moscow and Tashkent institutes, performing translated Russian and European classics alongside newly commissioned national repertoire. In Osh that repertoire runs in Kyrgyz and Uzbek.

Look for

How the figures are arranged — Soviet monumental art has a consistent grammar of gesture: the raised arm, the forward gaze, the group in profile advancing left to right. Once you see it you cannot unsee it, and you will spot it again on the mountain museum.

5

The sculpture marks the thing that made Osh: its position at the western end of the passes from Kashgar. Caravans coming out of the Tarim Basin over the Terek and Irkeshtam routes came down into the Fergana Valley, and Osh was the first substantial place to stop, water, trade and rest.

What actually moved

Less silk than the name implies. The traffic was horses (the Fergana Valley's famous "heavenly horses" were an object of Chinese imperial desire), slaves, metals, paper, jade, textiles and fruit. And relays of ideas: Buddhism moved east along these roads, Islam moved east behind it, and Turkic languages spread west. Very few merchants travelled the whole length; goods changed hands at towns like this one, dozens of times.

Note

Map pin is approximate — follow the guidebook route down Abdykadyrov toward the river and you will not miss it.

6

Green, shaded, running alongside the Ak-Buura — "white camel" — with a fairground, ice cream sellers and the whole city out walking in the evening. Take the guidebook's advice literally: get an ice cream and sit for twenty minutes. This is the sociable heart of Osh.

The name

Alisher Navoi (1441–1501) was a poet and statesman at the Timurid court in Herat, and the figure who made Chagatai — the Turkic literary language ancestral to modern Uzbek — a serious vehicle for high literature, arguing in print that it could do everything Persian could. He is the foundational figure of the Uzbek literary canon, and Uzbekistan claims him accordingly.

So a large central park in a Kyrgyz city named after him is not a neutral piece of municipal naming. It's an acknowledgement of the depth of Uzbek cultural presence in Osh — which predates the border and is a permanent feature of the place.

Practical

The fairground has its own hours and may not run every day. Entry to the park is free.

7

The bazaar is the oldest institution in Osh and arguably the only one that has run continuously since antiquity — a long ribbon of trade strung out along the Ak-Buura, doing exactly what it did when the caravans came over the passes. Your guidebook notes it was relocated in 2025 after flood damage, so what you find may be newer than the history suggests. Ask a local where it's currently trading; the answer may differ from the map.

What to look for

Osh knives (pichok) are the city's signature craft — hand-forged blades with bone or horn handles, made in workshops in the mahalla and sold here. Also: dried apricots and raisins from the valley, spice pyramids, tandyr-baked non bread stamped with a patterned seal, felt kalpaks, and cradles.

How to do it

Cash in small som notes; money changers work the roadside. Bargaining is normal and should stay friendly. Ask before photographing stallholders — a nod usually does it. Go hungry: samsa straight out of a clay oven is the best thing you will eat today.

8

Small, low, and easy to underrate. This is a Khanate-period building from the 1500s, which makes it the oldest mosque still standing in the city — four centuries of continuous prayer in one room. The highlight is the carved wooden doorway; the interior was whitewashed in a later restoration and lost much of its original surface, which locals will tell you about with some feeling.

Note the type. This is not the domed, tiled, monumental mosque of Samarkand or Bukhara. It's the Fergana Valley vernacular: timber columns, a deep shaded verandah, human scale, built for a neighbourhood rather than a dynasty.

Visiting

Shoulders and knees covered; women should carry a scarf for the head. Shoes off at the threshold. Avoid arriving at prayer times, especially Friday around midday. Ask before entering and before photographing, and never photograph people praying. If the main hall is locked, the courtyard is usually still worth the stop.

Note on the name

Guidebooks vary here. The 16th-century mosque at the foot of the mountain is generally called Rabat Abdulkhan (Ravvat Abdullakhan), while the mosque named for Muhammad Yusuf Baykhoji ogly is a separate, much later building nearer the bazaar. The map link goes to the 16th-century one, which matches the date and the position on your map.

9

A mahalla is not just a neighbourhood, it's a social institution. The physical form is distinctive and you'll see it immediately: high blank walls to the street, no windows facing out, a single gate per household opening into a courtyard with a vine, a raised platform for sitting and eating, and rooms arranged around it. Life turns inward.

The social form matters more. Mahallas run their own committees, mediate disputes, organise the collective labour and expense of weddings and funerals, and function as a mutual-aid network. Under the Soviets they persisted quietly beneath the official structures; they are still how much of daily life is organised here.

The craft

Knife-making workshops operate in these lanes — small forges producing the Osh pichok by hand. If you find one open it's a genuinely good look at a living trade, and buying direct from the maker is the right way to get one.

Please

This is where the 2010 violence hit hardest, and much of what you see was rebuilt afterwards. It is also simply people's homes. Walk through rather than linger, keep the camera down unless invited, and greet people — assalomu alaykum goes a long way. The map pin is approximate; the quarter is the destination, not a point.

10

The excavated ruins here are a hammam from the 11th century, the Karakhanid period, when Osh was a real town of a real Islamic state — bath complexes were standard urban infrastructure, with hypocaust heating under the floors. It's a good reminder that this city's Islamic history is a thousand years deep and not a recent overlay.

The mountain

Sulaiman-Too became Kyrgyzstan's first UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009, listed as a sacred landscape rather than a monument. People have been coming here to worship since at least the Bronze Age; over a hundred petroglyph sites have been catalogued on its slopes, and the ridge holds a network of caves and shrines that have been in continuous ritual use for millennia.

The Islamic layer came later. The name attaches the hill to the Prophet Sulaiman — Solomon — and one of its peaks is called his throne, Takht-i-Sulaiman. What you'll actually see is folk practice sitting on top of much older mountain worship: pilgrims rubbing against particular rocks, sliding down a stone worn glassy by centuries of bodies, tying cloth to branches, circling shrines. It's associated with fertility, back pain and healing. Some say three pilgrimages here equal a hajj — a claim orthodox clergy firmly reject, and an argument that is still live.

Practical

Small entry fee for the mountain (recently around 70 som), separate from the museum. Modest dress. These are worshippers, not an attraction — watch from a distance and don't photograph people at the shrines.

11

Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur was born in 1483, descended from Timur on his father's side and Genghis Khan on his mother's, and inherited the Fergana Valley at twelve when his father died in an accident involving a collapsing dovecote. At about fourteen he had a small retreat — a hujra — built up here, to pray and to look at his valley.

What happened next

He lost Fergana. He took Samarkand and lost that too, twice. Pushed out of Central Asia entirely, he went south, took Kabul in 1504, and in 1526 won the battle of Panipat and founded the Mughal Empire — which ruled most of the Indian subcontinent until the British ended it in 1857, and which produced the Taj Mahal, Fatehpur Sikri and a good deal of what the world now thinks of as Indian imperial culture.

He also wrote the Baburnama, one of the great autobiographies in any language: candid, observant, funny, full of notes on fruit and birds and his own failures. Osh appears in it, and he writes about this hill with real affection — he had, he says, seen nothing better.

What you're looking at

The present building is a reconstruction; the earlier structure was destroyed in the twentieth century. The view is the same one Babur was writing about, which is the actual point of coming up.

12

Your guidebook says most visitors skip it. Go anyway, but go for the container rather than the contents. A glass-and-concrete arch seals a natural cave mouth, and behind it a series of halls were blasted and cut into the rock. It opened in 1978, timed to the city's claimed three-thousandth anniversary.

Why it's interesting

Think about what it is: an officially atheist state carved a museum of scientific and archaeological explanation directly into the side of a working pilgrimage mountain, while pilgrims continued climbing past the entrance. It's not subtle. Soviet policy toward Central Asian sacred sites oscillated between suppression, neglect, and this — absorption, reframing the holy mountain as heritage and natural history.

Expect

Displays on regional archaeology, petroglyphs, pre-Islamic belief and local wildlife, many of them reproductions, with signage largely in Kyrgyz and Russian. Small fee, roughly 70 som. Hours are approximately 9:00–18:00 with a lunch closure; related museums on the mountain are closed Mondays, so check the door. On a hot afternoon the cave temperature alone justifies the ticket.

13

The walk ends here, on the western flank. If you have any energy left, the thing to do is reverse the logic: don't leave. Get back up onto the ridge for the last hour of light, when the heat goes, the whole Fergana Valley opens out to the west, and the call to prayer comes up at you from several mosques at slightly different moments.

Getting back

Ride-hailing apps work in Osh and are cheap. If you're flagging a car on the street, agree the price first. The city centre is a short ride from here.

Then eat

You are in the best food region in Kyrgyzstan and it isn't close. Osh plov is its own school, distinct from the Tashkent and Samarkand versions. Also: laghman hand-pulled to order, samsa from the tandyr, and shashlik. Traditionally plov is a lunch dish here — if you want the proper version, plan it for the middle of the day rather than the evening.

Worth knowing · the climb

Sulaiman-Too is not a hard hike but it is a real one: stone stairways, some steep sections, limestone polished slick by several thousand years of feet, and almost no shade. Reckon on 30–45 minutes up at a steady pace. Do it early morning or in the last two hours before sunset — the middle of an August day in the Fergana Valley is genuinely punishing. Take more water than feels necessary.

Walking it today

  • Heat. Osh sits lower and hotter than Bishkek — the Fergana Valley in August regularly runs into the high thirties. Split the walk: city stops in the morning, mountain in the evening. Or start at 16:00 and finish on the ridge at sunset.
  • Dress. Noticeably more conservative than Bishkek. Covered shoulders and knees make everything easier, and are expected at the mosque and appreciated on the mountain. Women: carry a scarf.
  • Photography. Architecture and views, no problem. Ask before photographing people — particularly women, stallholders, and anyone at a shrine. Never photograph people praying.
  • Museums. Most close Mondays. The Cave Museum runs a lunch break. Check the door rather than the internet.
  • Cash. Som in small notes for entry fees, the bazaar, ice cream and taxis. Money changers cluster along the bazaar road.
  • Language. Kyrgyz, Uzbek and Russian are all in daily use. Russian is the practical bridge. Assalomu alaykum works everywhere and is worth learning.
  • Water. Carry it. Refill before the climb, not during — there's little on the ridge.
  • 2010. Don't open a conversation with it. Read the monuments, and let people bring it up if they want to.
